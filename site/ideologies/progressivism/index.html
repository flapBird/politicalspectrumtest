Progressivism

Progressivism is the politics of deliberate improvement: identify injustice — in workplaces, politics, or institutions — then use organized government, expertise, and citizen action to fix it. It runs on the optimism that institutions can be made fairer on purpose.

Core beliefs

Injustice is a design problem — institutions can be rebuilt
Government action and regulation to correct market failures
Equality of opportunity as a core measure of fairness
Expertise and evidence in public policy
Expanding political participation; curbing concentrated power
Social change should be pursued actively, not left to time

Key figures

Theodore Roosevelt · United States, 1858–1919

Busted trusts, protected wilderness, and made the presidency an instrument of reform.

Jane Addams · United States, 1860–1935

Turned settlement houses into engines of social reform; won the Nobel Peace Prize.

John Dewey · United States, 1859–1952

Reimagined education as preparation for democratic participation.

Progressivism — questions, answered

What is progressivism in simple terms?

Society’s problems can be fixed on purpose. Rather than waiting for tradition or markets to sort things out, progressives use government, expertise, and organizing to actively reduce injustice and improve institutions.

How is progressivism different from liberalism?

Liberalism protects individuals from the state; progressivism also uses the state to protect individuals from each other and from concentrated power. Most progressives are also liberals — the difference is emphasis and appetite for intervention.

Is progressivism left or right?

Center-left: typically center-left on economics, liberty-leaning on civil liberties, and strongly progressive on social change.

What was the Progressive Era?

Roughly 1890–1920 in the United States: trust-busting, food and drug regulation, labor laws, women’s suffrage, direct primaries, and the income tax — reform led by figures like Theodore Roosevelt and Jane Addams.

Why do progressives trust experts?

Because the era’s wins — sanitation, regulation, public health — came from applying expertise to public problems. Critics answer that expertise can drift from democratic accountability; modern progressives try to pair the two.

What do progressives want today?

The mix varies by country: campaign-finance and lobbying reform, stronger labor rights, antitrust, climate action, universal services, and criminal-justice reform are common threads.

Which parties are progressive?

In the US, the progressive wing sits inside the Democratic Party; elsewhere, green, left-liberal, and social-democratic parties carry progressive agendas, often under other names.

What are the main criticisms of progressivism?

That reform movements commit the same overreach they fight (early progressivism had nativist strands), that technocracy sidelines democratic choice, and that constant institutional redesign has costs. Progressives reply that the status quo is not neutral.
